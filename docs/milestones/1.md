# Milestone 1: JSBSim as the game's core, headless

The plan below was approved by the owner on 20 November 2026, before any work started, after three rounds of his questions; their answers are in it. Its "done" lines are not changed after a result is seen. The results are added at the end when the milestone closes, with any failed line kept.

## The approved plan

## Context

Milestone 0 left a shell on the phone and JSBSim in a Worker that can load the Cessna and time a step. Nothing flies. Milestone 1 makes JSBSim the engine of a game, with no picture yet:

- a physics clock in the Worker, 120 steps a second, that does not slow when the screen does
- the player's controls going in, the airplane's state coming out
- the ground the game will draw, fed to JSBSim as a height patch that follows the airplane
- contact points, the game rules (fuel, still air, crashes, crash override) and the assists (neutral-tilt trim, automatic feet, nosewheel steering)
- a checkride: `c172x` and `f16` take off, fly a circuit and land, headless, and the results match native JSBSim within lines written below

**Done when (from `CLAUDE.md`):** `c172x` and `f16` take off, fly a circuit and land headless, with checkride results matching native JSBSim within the written tolerances.

Nothing new appears in the app except lines on the developer overlay. No tilt, picture, HUD or Three.js: those are milestone 2.

## The owner's five questions (20 November 2026), and what changed

**1. Flat or round Earth?** Both, each where it is simplest. **The game is flat:** the map, the tiles, the picture, where the jet is, all in plain north and east from the Honolulu numbers, as in Wings. Nothing you see or touch is round. **JSBSim is round inside,** because that is how it is built: its Earth can be resized or stopped from turning in an aircraft file, but it is always a globe (Verified, [FGInertial.cpp](https://github.com/JSBSim-Team/jsbsim/blob/v1.3.3/src/models/FGInertial.cpp)). Flattening it would mean changing JSBSim, which is the one thing this project does not do. So one small translator (`world.js`) sits between them: it turns "so far north, so far east" into the latitude and longitude JSBSim wants, and back. Think of flying on a chart: the chart is flat, the Earth isn't, and nobody notices over 200 miles.
- Cost on the phone: nothing you could measure. The round-Earth sums are already inside the 7.7 millionths of a second a step takes.
- What it does to the flying: nothing a pilot can feel.
- You were right that flat is the easier thing to solve, and that is the part we solve. The round part is solved already, by JSBSim.
- **The map's small scale error, and why it doesn't matter** (your follow-up). Any flat map of a globe is off somewhere; a sectional is too. Ours is exact north-south everywhere, and exact east-west at one latitude.
  - **The ground is not stretched.** The picture is drawn from the flat map exactly as Wings drew it. JSBSim draws nothing.
  - **The range to a nav point is not misleading.** It is measured on the map, the same map you see, as in Wings.
  - **The one real effect:** flying east or west, the jet crosses the map a little slower or faster than its airspeed says. Headings are true.
  - For scale: the channels between the islands were shortened by tens of miles on purpose. This is a hundred times smaller.
- **Where the exact latitude goes** (your second follow-up): **the middle of the map, as you said.** It makes no new problem. The map's origin stays on the Honolulu numbers; only the one number that sets the east-west scale moves, from Honolulu's latitude to the map's middle (about 20.6° north). The worst case drops from 1.4% to 0.9% (4 knots in 500, at the far north and south ends), and Honolulu goes from exact to 0.5% (a 12,000 ft runway is 60 ft different to JSBSim, which nothing shows). I had no good reason to prefer Honolulu; the plan now uses the middle.

**2. What's a crash?** There are four ways to call it, and no industry standard for a game.
| Way | Who | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How fast you close with the ground** at the touch | Wings; `CLAUDE.md` | A number off the VSI ("I hit at 1,600 feet a minute"). One rule for a runway and a hillside. Doesn't depend on how JSBSim's springs are tuned | One number per airplane to choose |
| g at the touch | JSBSim's Camel (20 g) | Simple | It measures the springs, not the arrival. Planning runs gave 190 g in one crash and a 43 ft bounce from a mere 600 ft/min in another |
| Load in each gear leg | FlightGear's Cessna | Closest to real: legs break one by one | Numbers per leg, per airplane; an engineer's rule, not a pilot's |
| Any touch of the airframe | FlightGear's Cessna | Simplest | Ends the flight on a wingtip scrape, which `CLAUDE.md` says must survive |

The real-world yardstick is how hard a landing the gear is built for: 10 ft/s (600 ft/min) for civil airplanes (Verified, 14 CFR 25.473); carrier jets like the F-4 roughly twice that or more (from memory and one secondary source; not confirmed). Wings' 8 m/s is 26 ft/s, 1,575 ft/min: more than two and a half times the civil figure, and about what I believe a carrier jet's gear is built to take. So **closing speed is the best answer for this game, and it is already your rule.** The plan keeps it, with Wings' number for the test aircraft. The F-4C's own number is a pilot question for milestone 3.

**3. Why Wings' feet and not opengeo-sim's?** They are the same idea: push the rudder against sideslip. Wings adds one thing: it looks a second ahead at where the sideslip is going, so the rudder leads the turn the way a pilot's feet do. I flew all of them on the JSBSim Cessna while planning (Supported): full stick into a 30° bank and back.
| Feet | Peak sideslip at 70 knots | At 100 knots |
|---|---|---|
| None | 6.5° | 4.0° |
| FlightGear's (rudder follows the aileron), at its better sign | 3.8° | 3.6° |
| opengeo-sim's (sideslip only) | 5.3° | 3.4° |
| Wings' (sideslip, looking a second ahead) | 2.2° | 1.0° |

So it wasn't reinvented in vain: theirs is the first half of Wings'. Wings' is the right pick because it measures best, you have flown it, and it works only through the rudder. PR 8 now prints this table as part of the checkride, so the choice stays on record.

**4. Will the trim make it a game or a fight?** First, the drift I mentioned, because I made a test sound like a feature. Nothing is added to imitate a pilot trimming. It is the phugoid: every airplane left alone trades speed for height and back in a slow porpoise, and JSBSim's airplanes do it because they are airplanes. Wings did it too: its own checkride shows 15 knots of speed wander, hands off, and you never noticed, because nobody flies hands off. My test lets go of the phone for minutes only to prove the trim is right. Ripping round the islands you will not meet it.

**What you do meet, with the trim set once, is the lean,** and after your last note I agree it is the right answer, not a cost. Tilting the phone a little forward when fast is the trim control. It needs nothing on the screen, it explains itself, it is what a real airplane with fixed trim does, and you have already flown it. Wings' own checkride: 16% forward tilt at 450 knots, 36% at 600. On JSBSim's airplanes it comes out gentler (from JSBSim's own trim, Supported): the F-16 trimmed for 375 knots needs 4% forward at 580 and 15% back at 195; the Cessna trimmed for 100 needs 3% forward at 120 and 7% back at 80.

**So the plan stays with `CLAUDE.md` as written: the trim is set once, the Wings way.** One number per airplane, nothing moving, nothing to tune.

**What I flew of your other idea, for the record.** A "good pilot's thumb": the trim always at JSBSim's own level-flight value for the present speed, so phone level means level at any speed. Phone level, throttle up 40 seconds and back, hands off:
| | Nose | Altitude |
|---|---|---|
| F-16, trim set once (Wings) | 22° up to 21° down | climbs 13,000 ft |
| F-16, trim follows the speed | 1° down to 2° up | within 430 ft |
| Cessna, trim set once | 8° up to 14° down | within 620 ft |
| Cessna, trim follows the speed | 23° up to 15° down | within 900 ft, slows to 48 knots |

(Supported: runs while planning, the Cessna with its elevator free play removed.) It is calmer on the jet when nobody is holding the phone, but that is not how the game is flown, and it costs more than it gives: it is worse on a propeller airplane, it needs a table by speed and by height for every airplane (made at the wrong height, it sank 1,950 ft in two minutes), and it takes away the lean, which is the cue. **It is not built.** If you agree, PR 1 writes it into `docs/ideas.md` with these numbers, so it is there if the F-4C ever makes you want it.

**5. What equals accurate?** No need to decide now, and nothing here chases decimals. Two different questions are in play:
- **This milestone: "is the JSBSim in the browser the same JSBSim?"** A wiring check done by the machine, costing you nothing. The lines are the size of a gauge: 1 knot, 1 foot of altitude, 3 degrees. Wherever native JSBSim can't agree with itself that closely, its own wander sets the line. The few "identical" lines are yes-or-no checks of a recording against itself, not a hunt for digits. I loosened the checkride table to the same gauge-sized lines after your question.
- **Milestone 3: "does the F-4C fly like an F-4?"** That is the conversation to have then. My proposal for it, from Wings: numbers a pilot would recognise, within what a pilot could read (about 5 knots, 5%, 10% on g and roll rate); feel numbers reported with no pass or fail; and when a number can't be met, one report in pilot terms and your decision, never a week of tuning.

## 1. How others solved this

### The Worker's clock
| What others do | Label |
|---|---|
| Glenn Fiedler, "Fix Your Timestep": fixed step, an accumulator, a cap on catch-up, draw between the last two states. [article](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/) | Verified |
| WebKit does not slow timers inside a Worker ("We don't throttle timers in worker threads"). It does slow the page's own timers to 30 ms in Low Power Mode, and caps the screen at 30 frames a second on purpose. [DOMTimer.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/page/DOMTimer.cpp), [Page.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/page/Page.cpp), [bug 215745](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=215745) | Verified (WebKit's source, today's; not a given iOS) |
| An open WebKit bug says timers ran about 30% long on iOS 17 in Low Power Mode. It doesn't say whether Workers are affected. [bug 272156](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=272156) | Verified that it is open; its effect on us is Reasoned, settled by the phone check |
| WebFlight (JSBSim in a Worker, 120 Hz, GitHub Pages): the Worker paces itself with a 2 ms timer, counts the steps due from its own clock, caps catch-up at a quarter second, and posts a snapshot after each batch. [fdm-worker.ts](https://github.com/rtnicolae/WebFlight/blob/HEAD/apps/web/src/fdm-worker.ts) | Verified |
| FlightDynamicsLab: a 4 ms timer in the Worker with Fiedler's accumulator. [worker.mjs](https://github.com/tkir97/FlightDynamicsLab/blob/HEAD/dist/worker.mjs) | Verified |
| cannon-es's Worker example does the opposite: the page asks for each step. [worker.html](https://github.com/pmndrs/cannon-es/blob/master/examples/worker.html) | Verified |
| FDM's loop is page-driven too: 239 steps in 2.01 s on the owner's iPhone, in a Safari tab, not in Low Power Mode. | Verified (his phone) |

**Choice.** The Worker keeps its own clock: a 4 ms timer that calls FDM's loop (`src/sim/loop.js`, unchanged) with the Worker's own time. The page sends controls when they change and receives a state after each batch of steps. Page-driven ticks are the fallback. **Both are measured on the owner's phone, with and without Low Power Mode, before the rest is built on one.** The rule for choosing is written in PR 3, and the one not chosen is taken out in PR 11.

### Where the game's world sits on JSBSim's Earth
| What others do | Label |
|---|---|
| JSBSim flies over a round, turning Earth and thinks in latitude, longitude and height. FlightGear's world is that same Earth, so it needs no mapping. [JSBSim.cxx](https://github.com/FlightGear/flightgear/blob/next/src/FDM/JSBSim/JSBSim.cxx) | Verified |
| Wings' world is flat: north and east in metres from the Honolulu 8R threshold, with the island groups moved closer together. Its tiles reach about 65 km north and 230 km south of the threshold (2° of latitude). | Supported (Wings' `index.json`, read while planning) |
| FDM's ground grid is laid out in latitude and longitude (the kind a map uses, geodetic), and checked against an independent calculation. A grid laid out in JSBSim's other latitude (geocentric) once landed 10 nm away. | Verified (native JSBSim) |
| JSBSim's official Unreal plugin ties a flat game world to JSBSim's Earth at one reference point. | From FDM's `RESEARCH.md`; not re-read for this plan |

**Choice.** The world is laid on JSBSim's Earth as a plain grid of latitude and longitude, with its origin on the real Honolulu 8R threshold. North is north and up is up everywhere, and a square of game posts is exactly the grid FDM's callback reads, with nothing resampled. The cost: east-west distances are true at one latitude and drift by 0.65% per degree of latitude away from it. That latitude is set in the middle of the map (about 20.6° north; the owner's choice), so the drift is 0.5% at Honolulu and 0.9% at the map's ends. That is a recorded departure from real; PR 4 measures it against JSBSim's own Earth.

### The ground: a height patch that follows the airplane
| What others do | Label |
|---|---|
| JSBSim asks a "ground callback" for the height, contact point and slope under the airplane and under each wheel, every step. Its own default is one height over a smooth Earth. [FGGroundCallback.h](https://github.com/JSBSim-Team/jsbsim/blob/v1.3.3/src/input_output/FGGroundCallback.h) | Verified |
| FlightGear installs its own callback and keeps a small "ground cache" around the airplane (wheel span plus two steps of travel), refilled every step. It does not start the flight model until the scenery near the start has loaded. [JSBSim.cxx](https://github.com/FlightGear/flightgear/blob/next/src/FDM/JSBSim/JSBSim.cxx), [fdm_shell.cxx](https://github.com/FlightGear/flightgear/blob/next/src/FDM/fdm_shell.cxx) | Verified |
| WebFlight and opengeo-sim write one height number each step. Every wheel then sees the same flat ground. opengeo-sim found that a sudden jump in that number "puts the gear inside the hillside; the spring then answers with a few million pounds", and that a stale grid gave a phantom runway. WebFlight found half-loaded tiles put the ground 48 km down. [jsbsim.js](https://github.com/jorisfalter/opengeo-sim/blob/HEAD/src/fly/jsbsim.js), [milestone-2.md](https://github.com/rtnicolae/WebFlight/blob/HEAD/docs/milestone-2.md) | Verified |
| FDM's callback reads a height grid compiled into the WebAssembly: true height and slope under each wheel. A flat grid equals JSBSim's own ground bit for bit; a 5° slope holds a parked Cessna. | Verified (native JSBSim) |
| FDM's grid can only be set before the aircraft loads (`SetGroundGrid: only before load`). A patch that follows the airplane needs it replaced in flight. | Verified (the committed C++) |
| "Sky Hopper", which `CLAUDE.md` names for this method: not found in public. The plan rests on FlightGear's method instead. | Not found |

**Choice.** FlightGear's method, with FDM's grid as the cache. The game cuts a patch of heights round the airplane from the same posts the picture will draw, and replaces it as the airplane moves. Patches are cut on the terrain's own post lines, so the ground under a wheel is the same number before and after a swap: no jump. If the next patch isn't available, the physics holds still; it never flies over ground that hasn't loaded. This needs one small change to the C++ host layer and a rebuild of the WebAssembly (PR 2).

Flown: 1,500 ft/min survives, 1,900 does not. | Supported (Wings' checkride) |

Whether you want that free play under your hands is a question for milestone 2, when you can fly it.

### What JSBSim already has for the game rules
Tried while planning, on both aircraft (**Supported**):
- **Engine running at the start:** `propulsion/set-running`, set before the start, works. Both sit at idle with the brakes on and move 0.03 ft in 13 seconds.

## 2. What gets built

All of it in `src/sim/`, with no DOM code, so the same files run in the Worker and in Node.

| Piece | What it is |
|---|---|
| The flight (`game.js`) | One flight = one fresh JSBSim, one aircraft, its assists, the rules, the ground patches. Each step: controls → assists → JSBSim → rules. A restart is a new flight |
| Aircraft settings (`aircraft/c172x.js`, `f16.js`) | Which JSBSim files it needs, which JSBSim control each of the seven game controls drives, neutral speed and trim, whose feet and whose steering taper, crash line, limits |
| The world (`world.js`) | The mapping above, and the test field (after Wings' `FIELD`: a flat 10,000 × 150 ft runway at sea level, laid like Honolulu's 8R: numbers at the world's origin, pointing 090 true) |
| Ground (`ground.js`) | Terrain sources (the flat field; a test terrain with a slope, a hill, a lake and sea, on 10 m posts like Hawaii's), and cutting patches from them |
| Assists (`assists.js`) | Neutral trim (set once), automatic feet, nosewheel steering. They write only stick, rudder, trim and steering commands |
| Rules (`rules.js`) | Fuel, touches, hard impact, water, crash override, broken state, limits reported |
| The clock (`clock.js`) and the Worker's messages | The Worker's own timer driving FDM's loop; fly, controls, state, pause, resume, restart |
| The checkride (`tests/checkride/`) | The pilot, the circuit, the recorder, the native replay, the table |

**Rules the design keeps**
- **A flight is a start and a tape.** The start is a list of values set before JSBSim's first step (position, engine running, fuel frozen, trim, gear, flaps, brakes). After that, everything the game does to JSBSim goes through one array of inputs, every step. A test fails if game code sets anything else after the start. That is what lets native JSBSim replay a flight, and it is FDM's fast path. (The review found why the start must carry the trim: fed only through the array, it arrives one step late and the Cessna's free play then holds its elevator short, at 81 knots.)
- **FDM's copied files change only where they must.** Each change is written into `tools/from-fdm.mjs`, which is the record and the check, and `provenance.json` follows from it in the same pull request.
- **JSBSim's data is not edited.** The test aircraft are flown as upstream ships them. `c172x` has no contact point on its nose or belly and `f16` none on its tail; that is recorded, not fixed. The F-4C's file gets all of them in milestone 3.
- **Versions.** Any pull request that changes a file the app loads (the list in `sw.js`) changes the version. The `f16`'s files are for the tests only and never join that list.
